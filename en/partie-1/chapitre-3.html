The Arena and the Arrows

That summer, I was seventeen years old, and Shabrān was receiving its neighbors as it had not done in years.

The delegations arrived over the course of three days, each one announced by a cloud of dust that could be seen rising on the horizon long before the banners could be made out — envoys from the small cities of the foothills, who came on horseback with a handful of men and modest gifts, and whole processions sent by the great kingdoms of the plain, loaded with chests that it took four porters to move. My father received everyone with the same solemnity, great and small alike, and I believe it was less a matter of courtesy than a way of reminding each of them of his proper place.

To the north, beyond the mountain passes, lay Kardun, a kingdom of stone and snow from which iron and horses were drawn, and which negotiated, year after year, the price of the grain we sold it. To the east, the small cities of Sarhad — three or four fortified towns clinging to the passes along the border — paid a light tribute in exchange for our protection against the raiders of the steppes; their chiefs came less to negotiate than to reaffirm, once a year, that they still belonged to the right side.

And then there were the kingdoms of the south, richer and older, which Shabrān had never needed to conquer or subdue. We traded with them, exchanged daughters in marriage, and renewed treaties out of habit more than necessity, in a balance that no one could have called anything but fragile.

The first day was nothing but splendor — tables loaded with food between the colonnades, spiced wine, courtesies exchanged cup in hand. Rustum, who hated that kind of evening, had whispered to me that real diplomacy never began until the wine had finished saying what politeness kept quiet.

He was right. It was the next day, at the negotiating table, that things began to go sour. I was not there — my rank did not open that door to me — but I caught the echo of it afterward from Ishirine, who had been allowed to sit apart, at the back of the room. There was talk of trade routes and of an old border dispute. Voices began to rise. One chief in particular — Bahram, from the land of Nakhur, wedged between Sarhad and the southern kingdoms, neither small enough to be content with its rank nor large enough to weigh on ours — flatly rejected my father’s terms and left the table before the meeting had ended.

On the third day, my father received all the delegations gathered together for the closing speech that traditionally came before their departure. He spoke at length, in an even voice, recalling the old ties between Shabrān and its neighbors, and the prosperity that peace had brought to them all. I watched him speak and thought, as I often did, that he never raised his voice because he had never needed to.

It was then that Bahram stood up.

He did not even wait for my father to finish. “You speak of old ties,” he said, loud enough for the whole room to hear, “but all I have seen at this table is one kingdom that decides and neighbors that accept. You speak of prosperity, but all I have seen is yours growing while mine stagnates in the shadow of your walls. They say Shabrān has the strength of its king. I wonder, looking at you, whether that strength has not begun to grow old with him.”

Silence fell over the room. I saw my brother — the one already counted as the future king — put his hand on the pommel of his sword. I saw Syrus take a step toward the dais. No one moved any further, because my father, with a single look, had been enough to freeze the whole room.

Bahram went on, calmer, as if he had prepared every word. He recalled — and it was true, I learned it only that day — that a very ancient right still allowed any chief of a neighboring land, at such a gathering, to propose a duel for the crown, which the king remained free to accept or refuse. Few had dared to do so to his face; most did it out of bravado, knowing their challenge would not be taken seriously, and went home with their pride intact and their own thrones still theirs. Bahram, for his part, phrased it as an accusation. “I challenge you,” he said. “In single combat. For your throne. If you refuse, everyone gathered here will know why.”

My father did not answer right away. He stayed silent for a long moment, standing on the dais, looking at Bahram the way one looks at a problem that must be solved before nightfall. Then he walked down as far as the balustrade overlooking the inner courtyard, from which the arena was usually shown to guests, and studied him at length from that height — the breadth of his shoulders, the way he carried his weight on his front leg, the impatience that showed beneath his show of stillness.

“Very well,” he said at last, without raising his voice. “The arena. Within the hour.”

The news spread through the city faster than the heralds themselves could have carried it. The market, for its part, went on living without a care — the foreigners who had come for the occasion crowded in mostly for the silks, with no interest at all in the palace’s affairs. Yasmine, that day, sold more rugs and figs than in an ordinary month, unaware that a few streets away from her stall, the king was preparing to fight for his crown.

The arena filled in record time. Rustum had dragged me along to a seat up high, near the royal stands. Ishirine joined us, her face closed — she hated that kind of spectacle, but would have hated even more to be kept away from it.

My father walked down into the arena without armor, in a plain tunic, a short sword in his hand. Bahram had kept his studded leather breastplate on and carried a much heavier blade, made for cleaving rather than piercing.

Before charging, he raised his weapon toward the stands, as if already addressing a crowd on his side. “Watch closely,” he called out, to no one in particular and to everyone at once. “Watch how it dies, a kingdom that believed peace would last forever.” Then he charged, without waiting for a reply — he wanted none.

From the very first exchanges, the fight looked like nothing I had seen Rustum teach me in the training yard. Bahram struck with raw power, every blow meant to break a guard rather than to land — and my father parried every time at the very last instant, constantly looking for the angle that would cost him the least energy.

There came a moment when everything seemed to tip over. Bahram strung together three strikes so close together that my father had to sacrifice his guard to protect his face — and his sword flew out of his hands, rolling in the sand three paces away from him. The crowd let out a roar. I stood up without realizing I had done so, and Rustum had to put a hand on my shoulder to make me sit back down.

“Watch closely,” he told me, without taking his eyes off the arena. “Word reached me once of an escort your father led with his contingent — supplies being carried to a neighboring village, as a pledge of good neighborliness between our two lands. On the way back, bandits were waiting for them in a narrow pass between two mountain flanks. Your father’s men, exhausted by the road and caught off guard, had no time to hit back: they found themselves surrounded, then disarmed one after another, by an enemy striking from the shadows. That enemy, sure he had the situation in hand, let himself be distracted by the loot, almost forgetting his opponents. Your father seized the moment. He unbalanced the distracted enemies, one after another, turning each weapon against its own bearer, and gave his men the breathing space they needed to get back on their feet. The fight changed sides before those bandits understood what was happening to them.”

In the arena, Bahram was already advancing on my unarmed father, blade raised, savoring every step. “Your people will remember this,” he said, loud enough to carry to the stands, no doubt believing he was speaking the last words anyone would hear from this duel. “They will remember the day Nakhur did justice to an old man too proud to give up his place.” He raised his weapon for what he took to be the finishing blow.

Rustum leaned toward me, his voice lower still, almost hurried. “Your father and I served in the same cavalry, each at the head of his own group. We were sent to relieve a remote village that its neighbors were about to annex. When we arrived, each on his own flank of the front, the village was already falling — that is the position from which we joined the battle. In front of each of us there were ten enemies, or fifty, or a hundred, who knows. We fought all night, until only a handful of us were left, worn down by wounds and exhaustion. The enemy thought itself victorious, confident in its numbers, and was preparing the assault that was meant to finish us all. Then your father got to his feet one last time. He tied a strip of cloth around his wounded arm to keep his weapon in hand regardless, and threw himself alone against the rest of the troops, cutting a path to their commander. Each of his blows was decisive; nothing seemed able to slow his advance. The enemy chief, the very one who had been flaunting his victory a little earlier, climbed onto his horse and charged him, a long spear in his hand — their clash raised a cloud of dust. Your father used that diversion to cut off his head in one clean stroke. That is who Bahram is dealing with.” He added nothing more. He had no need to.

My father did not give any more ground. As Bahram’s blade came down, he pivoted on his back leg, caught his opponent’s wrist in mid-motion, and tore the weapon from his hands in the same twist. Bahram, in turn, found himself disarmed.

My father did not keep the blade he had just taken. He threw it into the sand, far away from them both, and waited, bare-handed, for Bahram to rush him bare-handed in turn. The blows that followed, my father took them all, without parrying a single one — to the face, the ribs, the shoulder — barely stepping back, as if he had decided, once again, to let a man wear himself out against him rather than stop him too soon.

Then, when Bahram’s last blow was no more than a limp gesture, spent of all strength, my father finally answered. A strike to the shoulder, another to the flank, a last one, with the flat of the hand, full in the face — and Bahram collapsed to his knees in the sand, stunned, arms hanging, his empty gaze fixed on a point that only he could see.

My father leaned toward him, one hand held out — to help him up, I believe, more than to finish him. He did not have time.

The sky darkened all at once, as if a cloud had just passed in front of the sun. Then came the whistling sound, hundreds of whistles layered on top of one another, and the first volley of arrows came down on the arena — straight at Bahram, still on his knees, still too dazed to understand what was happening to him. My father had no time to save him. He had only enough time to step back, and to see Bahram’s body stiffen under the impacts, once, twice, three times, before slumping for good into the sand.

The second volley followed almost immediately — the last one, I learned afterward — and that one was aimed at my father. He had no shield. He had, once again, only time for a reflex older than thought: he seized Bahram’s lifeless body and lifted it in front of him, just in time for the shafts to bury themselves there in his place. When the volley ceased, he let the body fall back into the sand and stood there, alone, unharmed, in the middle of the silence.

The crowd had scattered in every direction, screaming. The guards had rushed toward the heights of the arena from which the shots seemed to come, swords drawn, hunting an enemy they could not find, as if chasing a ghost in a night at high noon. They searched the stands, the rooftops, the service corridors. They found footprints, a bow abandoned in a recess, nothing more. Not a face, not a name, not a trail that led anywhere.

Bahram had died on Shabrān’s soil, mere moments after losing, fairly, the fight that he had himself demanded. My father stood in the very middle of the arena, hands empty, covered in dust and in blood that was not his own. He said nothing. He simply raised both hands, palms open toward the stands, and stayed that way for a long moment.

The delegations, who left the very next day, voiced no accusation. But I saw, in the way we were bid farewell on that last morning — the shorter bows, the gifts that had ceased to be offered — that something had closed which would not easily reopen. No one accused us of anything. They simply no longer quite believed us.

In the weeks that followed, the caravans stopped coming. Not all at once — first one, then two, then a prolonged silence where there had been mingled tongues and endless haggling. The treaties renewed that summer were never officially broken. They were simply left to die, the way a fire is left to go out when no one throws wood on it anymore.

My father summoned his council almost every evening. Syrus reinforced the northern garrison without having been given an express order. As for me, I went on going to the market. Yasmine still paid me a little too much. Rustum kept correcting my footing in the training yard, never once speaking of Bahram again.

Nothing, to all appearances, had changed.
