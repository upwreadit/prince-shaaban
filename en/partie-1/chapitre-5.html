The Bodies and the Blade

About 12 minutes’ reading

The morning did not come all at once. It slipped in among the fires like a thief afraid of making a sound, and the light it brought lit nothing that anyone wanted to see.

I had been walking for hours — or perhaps for just one, I no longer know. My hands no longer quite belonged to me; they still carried the weight of those I had pulled from the rubble. The city was still burning in places, but the screaming had stopped. What was left was an after-silence, a lull and not an ending, though I did not know that yet. With every step, I raised a dust that no longer smelled of leather and cinnamon.

I did not try to go back to Rustum’s body. The quarters of the Common Folk were nothing now but a tangle of ruins where I no longer even recognized the streets, and in the state the city was in, I had eyes only for those who still had a chance of being saved.

I do not remember deciding to walk toward the palace. I only remember that my feet carried me there.

On the way, I passed other figures whom the night had not finished off. One of them, an old man sitting against a section of wall, his cane broken in two at his feet, held out a trembling hand to me, barely managing to say: “Water…” I had almost nothing left in my flask, but I poured out what remained, helped him sit up straighter, and left him there, a little less alone than before, before taking up my road again. It was neither the first nor the last hand I held that night, and I did not have the strength to linger on this one any more than on the others.

The approaches to the palace bore the marks of a ferocious fight: scorched sections of wall, broken spear shafts planted in the earth like dead trees, bodies that no one had yet had time to move. The great gates hung half torn from their hinges.

I was recognized before I even had to say a word — a guard, his face gray with dust, who took me by the arm without explanation and led me through the wreckage of the main courtyard. I did not ask where he was taking me. I think I already knew, without wanting to admit it to myself.

He led me to a corner of the courtyard where several bodies lay apart from the others, each under its own white shroud. I had to uncover them myself, one shroud after another, without knowing in advance what I would find beneath.

I knelt before the first one and lifted its shroud.

It was Syrus. He was still holding his hammer, the handle broken halfway along; his breastplate bore four gashes, and his face, frozen, seemed to be searching for something above him. Something in me gave way, for the first time.

I moved on to the second shroud. Baam. His shield was still strapped to his arm, dented by blows he must have taken in someone else’s place. What had given way in me cracked a little further, and I sensed that I would not have the strength to last to the end if I stopped to let it happen.

One last shroud remained. I lifted it.

My father. He was still holding his sword, the hilt gripped so tightly in his hand that no one could have torn it from him, even dead.

I knelt before the three of them, unable to link these three bodies with the three men who had taken up all the room in the house where I had grown up.

A dizziness seized me, kneeling before them, worse than the one I had known facing Rustum. Rustum, a few hours earlier. My father, just now. And if my brother, if Ishirine had vanished too that night — what would be left of me to fill such a void? What had I done to bring such a fate down upon myself?

One thought took shape, cold, almost soothing in its simplicity: I would share this suffering with those who had inflicted it on me. I would carve into them the same emptiness they had carved into me. I would destroy everything they cared about, as they had destroyed everything I cared about.

I do not know how long I stayed like that, my hands on my head, my mind in total disarray, on the verge of breaking for good.

It was a sound behind me that made me turn my head, with a hope I did not yet dare name.

And I saw her.

Ishirine. Alive. Disheveled, a cut on her arm still bleeding, her dress torn and stained with blood that was not her own — but alive. I stood up without thinking and held her against me so hard that she let out a small, painful laugh, the kind of laugh you have when you are too afraid to cry.

“I’m fine,” she breathed against my shoulder. “I’m fine, Shaaban.”

As I drew back from her, I saw the blade she had not let go of — thin, with a plain hilt, without ornament, made to kill and not to shine. It was not a discovery for me: how many nights had I watched her train in secret, behind the gardens of the west wing; how many times had I stood guard while she corrected her stance. But seeing her like this, that same blade covered in the blood of that night, was quite different from seeing her practice against a bale of straw.

“Our father never knew,” she said, following my gaze.

I did not have the heart to tell her what I had always thought: that a king notices everything that happens behind the walls of his own palace, and that he had perhaps chosen, in silence, to see nothing.

Our brother found us there, both of us, in front of the bodies. He was still wearing his battle armor, notched in several places; part of his face was still reddened by the blood of a cut on his forehead, which had bled a great deal before beginning to close. He stopped in front of our father and stood there for a long moment, without a gesture, without a word.

The three of us sat down beside our dead, and it was Ishirine who spoke first.

“I was not far from the cavalry quarters that evening, with two maids — I was coming back from one of my escapades, the ones where I slipped out to watch the soldiers train without being seen. The neighboring lands had already made their presence known at the gates of the kingdom when everything tipped. We had not been wary of familiar faces: spies, infiltrated for months into the highest circles, disguised as trusted people, struck from the inside at the very moment the outer alarm sounded — the Court, the palace, the garrison, the cavalry, all at once. The panic was immediate, and total.

“My maids and I were separated within moments. Each of us looked for shelter on her own; I do not hold it against them, I would probably have done the same if I had had somewhere to go. The invaders killed without effort, and all around me there was nothing but blood and screams. I had no choice but to run, and it was while running that I found myself near the cavalry, where the fighting was already raging.

“Syrus and Baam were talking not far from there before the attack — about running the kingdom, I imagine, those were always their subjects — and they recognized me from afar, by my dress. Only the dress could give me away like that. They agreed in a few words: Baam headed for the granaries, weapon in hand, bow on his back; Syrus gathered men to cut a path to me.

“I did not wait for someone to come and get me. I hid, slipped through, avoided what I could avoid, and ran when there was nothing else to do. I came to a wall, at the exit of the gardens, with no way out at all — the fighting there had already grown too large for me to turn back. There was a tree, with branches too low to be safe. I climbed it anyway, and just as I was about to jump to get over the wall, I missed my footing.

“I fell.

“I remember the ground under my hands, and the blades already approaching, too fast for me to dodge in that position. I knew it was over. Then arrows burst from a window above our heads — a shoulder, a thigh, a hand, I could not tell you how many were hit. And Syrus came over the wall from the top, in a movement I will never forget: he grabbed two men and crushed their heads, one after the other, the way you would crack nuts.

“He unhooked his hammer from his belt, gave his orders to secure the area, and turned to me. I had had the fright of my life. ‘We destroyed all the enemies behind this wall on our way here,’ he told me. ‘Take that path and go to the granary — you will find your brother there, he will know what to do. Here, let us take over.’

“I took that path. Enemy soldiers already lay here and there, cut down by Syrus’s group, and the smell of smoke grew stronger as I approached the granaries.

“The granaries were burning. Someone had set fire to them. And not far away, Baam’s body already lay lifeless — it looked as if he had fallen into an ambush, as if he had been waited for there.

“I knelt before him. I cried out — cries of pain that sounded like nothing, because the tears, for their part, would not come. And those cries attracted what should not have been attracted: enemies, not far off, thirsty for blood. I heard them approaching. I did not need to turn around to know there were three of them.

“Without getting up, I took a knife from what Baam was carrying. I gave them neither a gesture nor a sound to betray me — a simple movement of the wrist, as discreet as if I had still been crying — and the blade left my hand before any of the three could believe that a girl kneeling in tears was anything other than easy prey. It lodged in the first one’s eye, and he died without understanding what had struck him. The other two froze, stunned to see their comrade fall — and by the time they understood, I already had my brother’s blade in hand. I slipped behind one of them, opened two quick wounds on his calf and knee, and cut his throat when he went down on one knee.

“The third was stronger than the other two together, and I was only a sixteen-year-old girl, frail, in a silk dress torn on every side. From the first exchanges, he overpowered me: I gave ground with every blow, I barely managed to deflect his blade with mine, and my arms were already trembling under the force of his attacks. I survived his first assaults more by luck than by skill, and I took advantage of a moment when he already thought himself the winner — sword raised above his shoulder, ready to come down on my back while I was still on my knees — to plant my blade in his foot. Only then did I have time to dodge his blow. Slowed by the pain, immobilized, he was no longer in a state to defend himself. I took Baam’s bow, and put three arrows in him before he could free himself.”

She finished her story without shedding a tear, but her voice, from beginning to end, had never stopped trembling with sadness — as if she were reliving it a second time, as strongly as the first.

Our brother waited for her to fall silent before speaking in turn, without turning his eyes away from our father’s body.

“I was with Father, in the palace infirmary, when they attacked. They came by the dozens, then by the dozens again. Neither he nor I had our armor on when the alarm sounded. They wanted nothing but destruction, and Father, as king, could not allow himself to abandon the infirmary before the reinforcements arrived. He gave the order to close the doors. I was to lead the evacuation of the sick a little farther away, while he held the others back behind that door.” He fell silent for a moment, his breath heavy. “I left him my weapon, before we separated.”

He sighed.

“And that is where we are.”

We stayed like that, the three of us, until the sun stood high in the sky, too empty to say anything more. What that night had taken from us, none of the three of us could have counted. What it left us, however, fit in a single sentence, which I only put into words years later: we were only three now, and we would have to learn to stand upright with that.

“Oh, my Prince. You have lost a father. I lost a part of my family, and the weight on my shoulders, that night, was heavier than you could imagine, if only you knew. I am not trying to soften you or to beg for your compassion. I only want you to understand where I come from, what I went through before becoming what I am. Let me tell you what comes next — perhaps you, too, will see more clearly.”
