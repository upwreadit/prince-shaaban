Part I · Chapter 1

The Weight of a Body

About 4 minutes’ reading

✦ ✦ ✦

Theme

Babylon was burning, consumed by fire and ash. Screams rose from the alleys, crossed the squares and were lost behind the houses in flames. Now and then a figure burst out of a flaming doorway, clothes devoured by the fire, while hands tried to put out the flames. The conqueror’s domination had reduced the city to chaos.

On his knees among the ruins, the Prince held the lifeless body of his father, King Sharaman, close against his chest. His hands were red and shaking; his mouth hung open, but no sound came out. Around him, the city kept burning. Yet the screams reached him from farther and farther away, as if muffled behind a thick wall of stone, until they vanished completely.

Then silence fell over Babylon.

A heavy, suffocating silence. The great ruler of Persia was nothing more than a motionless body, his nobility fading under the grime of war. The blood had almost dried on the Prince’s fingers. He lowered his head. The world had just torn someone he loved away from him, without warning, without even the courtesy of a goodbye.

Deep inside, in the darkest folds of his consciousness, I was watching the scene.

Ordinarily, I would have laughed. I would have mocked his weakness, spat on those futile tears blurring his sight, and I would have demanded that he get up, weapon in hand, and slit the throats of those who stood in our way. But faced with this broken body, faced with this absolute collapse, my bitterness failed me. The mockery died in my throat, smothered by an echo too old, too familiar. His grief no longer appeared to me as a flaw to exploit; it was turning into a mirror I had no desire to look into.

“Cry, my Prince,” I whispered in his mind, in a voice I no longer recognized. “I don’t blame you. I know the weight of finding a father lifeless.”

I fell silent for a moment, then added:

“I know a far greater weight still… If only you knew.”

He did not push me away. Grief had left him drained of all strength, open to my own melancholy. I had no wish to justify myself for what I had become, nor to extract the slightest pity from him. But his pain broke the locks on my memory, and an ancient image rose up behind Sharaman’s: a man whose teachings I still carried, then another face, closer, harder to bear.

The coppery smell of Sharaman’s blood flooded the senses we shared. It clung to skin, clothes and stone. Then another smell answered it, torn from the very depths of my memory. The gray dust of the Babylonian ruins began to prickle my nostrils before turning into golden sand, burning hot, carried by a wind from another time.

It was no longer the heavy air of a dying city that I was breathing, but the dry breath of my youth.

The taste of dust slowly gave way to the taste of spices. The darkness of Babylon faded before the distant glow of a forgotten city. The wind carried the smell of tanned leather and of wool rugs being beaten in the sun. Somewhere, a hammer kept striking copper at steady intervals. Farther along, a woman was playing with some children; their laughter crossed the street and mingled with the merchants’ calls.

Babylon’s silence slipped further away. In its place returned simple, familiar sounds I had never thought to hold on to.

“Let me tell you, my Prince. Long before I was the stowaway in your mind, long before I was this Shadow you hate, I had a body of flesh. I had a name. I had a family.”

The memory took shape around me: an alley, market stalls, sunlight on the awnings. Among the people pressing through, a child walked without hurry. He knew these faces. Some turned toward him as he passed; he answered them with a gesture, then stopped in front of a merchant as if he had all the time in the world.

“It was in Shabrān, the kingdom of Night. Back there, I was Shaaban.”
