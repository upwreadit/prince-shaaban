The Fourth Son

I was born under the moon of Shaaban, the eighth moon of the calendar, the one that comes before the fast — and it is from that moon that I take my name, or so I have always been told. I am said to have come into the world without a cry, so silent that the midwife grew worried, and it supposedly took several vigorous efforts before I finally made up my mind to cry. The whole room is said to have rejoiced at that first cry — the whole room, except my father, who stood unmoved as marble in a corner, true to himself.

I cannot swear that things really happened that way. I did not live it myself, of course — it was my nurse who told me, twenty times perhaps, with enough conviction that I ended up believing it a little myself, too. The rest, however, I lived with my own eyes.

Shabrān smelled of leather and cinnamon. From the first light of dawn, the rugs beaten on the terraces released a golden dust that hung over the alleys for a long time before settling back on the stalls. The smiths struck copper at steady intervals, like a pulse beneath the skin of the city. And everywhere, there were voices: merchants praising their dates and spices, children chasing each other between the hooves of the donkeys, beggars who knew by heart the face of those who gave and the face of those who looked away.

A weaver from the south sold his rolls of silk next to a potter from the north who did not speak a word of our language and made herself understood through a flurry of smiles and gestures; two children argued over a fig, laughing, indifferent to everything else around them. Every language of the kingdom was spoken there, and a few more that had come from farther away, all mixed together into a murmur that nobody really tried to untangle. The tax collectors, for their part, truly got lost in it: it was enough for a merchant to suddenly pretend he no longer understood a word of the common tongue, just as he was counting out his coins, for the discussion to drag on until evening. I even saw some of the collectors change accents depending on who they were talking to, with a boldness that very nearly commanded respect.

Not far from where the tax collectors were arguing, that day, there was Idris, an old water carrier whose back bent a little more each year under the weight of his waterskins. He crossed the market at fixed hours, from the well in the lower quarter up to the houses too high for water to climb on its own, and nobody really knew anymore how many years he had been walking that road — he himself, I think, had ended up losing count. I used to help him lift his waterskins on the days his knees failed him, and in return he would tell me stories of the old city, the one even my father had not known: the walls that had not been rebuilt yet, the wells dug with bare hands, the winters so harsh that people burned the roof beams to keep from freezing. “You listen as if it still mattered,” he would sometimes say to me, with a toothless smile. I answered that yes, it did matter, and I think it really was true.

There was also Nadir, the apprentice of the blacksmith on the corner, a skinny boy of about ten years old when I met him, who spent his days working the bellows for a master who spoke to him only to correct him. I often stopped by his forge, less for the heat than for him: we traded a few blows, hidden from the smith, both of us happy to be able to do it now and then. He took more hits than he landed, but always kept an eye on the door, ready to grab the bellows again at the slightest sound of footsteps. “One day, I’ll be stronger than the master,” he would tell me in a low voice, like a secret you do not dare say too loud. I took him at his word.

Few people, in that crowd, knew who I really was. The regulars, those I had helped for years, had come to guess, without ever saying it out loud — as if it suited them as much as it suited me. The others took me for one young man of good family among so many others, and that confusion suited me very well.

I knew that city better than I knew the palace.

It was split into two worlds that crossed paths without really mixing. First came the Upper Quarters: houses of pale stone clinging to the cliffs, irrigated gardens, alliances sealed around tables piled too high with food. That was my world too, the one in which I was born, where I was dressed in silks I hated wearing. Then, down below the walls, the Common Folk: lanes of packed earth, roofs that leaked at the first rain, hands that worked from dawn to dusk without ever putting anything aside. Between the two, no open hatred — only a distance so old that people had long since come to think it natural. As for me, I had gotten into the habit of going back and forth between them as if they were one. Among the High Nobility, nobody really worried about it: I was the fourth son.

The palace, for its part, ran on an entirely different rhythm. Days were organized by audiences and bells, each hour finding its assigned place before it even began; people walked with measured steps, spoke in low voices, and the slightest delay stood out like a stain on a pale cloth. My three brothers did not have that freedom. Syrus, the eldest, had commanded the northern garrison since he had outgrown practice weapons; he wore a war hammer at his belt the way others wear a sword, and no one ever saw him without it. He was rarely seen to smile, but the men he commanded gave him a loyalty that I never once saw falter. Baam, the second, managed the kingdom’s granaries and trade routes with an almost obsessive rigor — it was said that he knew the exact amount of grain each valley produced before the harvest was even brought in. And then came the one who stood right before me in the order of blood, the one on whom the idea of the throne already weighed, silently. I crossed paths with him more often than I truly saw him: a hurried figure crossing a gallery, a voice one heard fading down a corridor, never quite present. He always seemed to be trying to prove something — to our father, to the dignitaries, to himself perhaps more than to anyone — and that race never truly came to an end, as if he feared that a moment of stillness would betray him.

Of Syrus, I mostly keep the memory of a morning when he let me, against all caution, ride behind him on his horse during an inspection of the garrison. He did not say a word during the ride, but when he set me back on the ground, he rested his hand on my head, for a second, before riding off without looking back. Of Baam, I remember an entire afternoon spent recounting the sacks in a warehouse with him, simply because I was bored and he had finally, with a sigh, accepted my help. He handed me his abacus with an almost comical seriousness, as if entrusting me with a matter of state. They were small moments, rare, but I remember them better than many entire days spent elsewhere.

My father was like them, only graver still. I once saw him receive, during an audience, a steward who had come to plead for leniency for a nobleman caught rigging his trade weights to avoid part of the taxes owed to the kingdom. The man spoke at great length — the culprit’s age, his service record, his family to feed. My father listened without a gesture, then answered in an even voice, as if ordering a meal: “Let him repay triple, and let him sit from now on in the last row of the council.” Nothing more. Not one word louder than another. And no one in that room thought for a moment of arguing with the sentence. That was his iron hand: not brutality, but the impossibility of answering back. He had governed Shabrān that way from the very beginning, and that kind of authority trembled before nothing, not even before his own sons. His justice remained swift, without apparent tenderness, but I never knew it to be unjust: he punished the fault, never the weakness. His schedule left him only a few hours for Ishirine and me, the two youngest. A servant told me, many years later, that he sometimes watched us from a high gallery, motionless and silent, as if trying to imagine a world where neither she nor I would have to carry what he carried every day. I never saw any of that myself. He never told us, anyway. I think, simply, that he did not know how.

That kind of gravity never quite left me, even when I was far from him — but the market had a strange power to lighten it, if only for a few hours, and I went back as soon as my days were my own.

It was there that I would stop almost every day at Yasmine’s, an old seller of rugs and figs whose stall had been leaning dangerously for years without her deciding to have it repaired. I would straighten her stakes, carry her rolled rugs and baskets of figs to her cart, and sometimes run her errands across the city when her legs hurt too much to walk. She always paid me a little too much, even when I refused — one more copper coin than the job was worth, or a handful of figs she knew I would give to someone else before nightfall.

“You’ll never change,” she would say, laughing, and she would push my coin back to me with a sharp gesture, the way you shoo away a fly.

It was my father who had entrusted my education to Rustum. Nobody in Shabrān really knew where that man with shoulders like rock and an already gray beard had come from: all we knew was that he had carried arms for a long time before entering my father’s service. His origins, and what had led him to my father, only my father seemed to know — a story neither of them ever told in front of me. Rustum taught me how to fight — footing, breathing, the exact moment to strike — but that was, I understood with time, only half of his lesson.

One day, when I came back from the market empty-handed, having given even my own meal to an old man I barely knew, he stopped me in the training yard, without raising his eyes from the blade he was sharpening.

“You’re at it again,” he said.

“He was hungry.”

“And you?”

“Less than him.”

He finished sharpening his blade before answering, as if the answer deserved to be taken slowly.

“You weren’t born for the throne. Good. The throne makes men blind. You, you can see.” He handed me a wooden staff worn down by years of blows. “So we’re going to make sure you keep seeing for a long time.”

That day, like every other, I walked away covered in dust and perfectly satisfied.

My sister, for her part, had found another path to the same freedom. Ishirine was the youngest of us, and already, in the palace corridors, people whispered the names of suitors she had never met. She seemed not to care about it at all. What truly occupied her, she showed to no one: she felt something growing in her that she herself still struggled to name, an ease in combat I had seen in no one else in the palace. She trained alone, at night, far from watching eyes, with a blade I never learned how she had come by. By day, she invented pretexts to linger near the cavalry, pretending to look for a servant or to have lost her way, just long enough to observe, memorize, and steal with her eyes the movements of the soldiers at training.

I discovered her one night when sleep would not have me. Rather than joining her right away, I slipped behind a cluster of jasmine, without making a sound — and I went back the following nights, several times, without ever letting myself be seen. I watched her strike a wooden pillar until her palms were scraped raw, repeat the same movement dozens of times when it did not satisfy her, struggle over footwork that Rustum had taught me to correct in just a few sentences.

It was only much later, one evening when I saw her stumble once too often on the same step, that I left the shadow of the jasmine. She had not heard me coming. I approached her, one step after another, without a sound — and each time she missed her move, my own foot shifted almost in spite of me, seeking the footing she should have taken, as if Rustum were still correcting me through her. It was by drawing closer like this, no longer thinking of hiding, that she finally felt she was no longer alone.

She spun around all at once, blade already raised — and only when she recognized me did she truly seem thrown off, her guard still high but her movement suddenly hesitant.

“If you say a word…” she began, not quite regaining her composure.

“Your back foot. It’s too far back.” Without waiting for her to answer, I showed her, once, the movement Rustum had made me repeat a hundred times before I truly understood it.

She lowered her weapon, bewildered, and watched me to the very end. Then she started again. The strike came out cleaner this time.

“Since when have you been spying on me?”

“For a while.”

“And who are you going to tell?”

“No one. But come back tomorrow: your wrist closes too early.”

A smile — the first real smile I had seen on her in weeks — split her face. From that night on, I came back regularly, never announced, always silent going and coming. I never said anything to anyone, not even to Rustum, and I think it is that shared silence, more than any oath, that made us allies.

That was my life, as it was back then: the laughter of the market, Rustum’s lessons, the stolen nights with Ishirine, my brother’s endless race through the corridors, and that iron hand of my father’s which, from afar, watched over us without ever showing it. The caravans from neighboring lands kept arriving loaded with spices and leaving loaded with wool; the treaties signed in the Upper Quarters kept their promises from one season to the next. At the market, Yasmine still paid me a little too much, Idris went on telling me his stories of a forgotten well, and Nadir watched for my passing to trade a few blows between two pumps of the bellows. In the training yard, Rustum kept on teaching me to see. And behind the gardens of the west wing, my sister corrected, night after night, what an entire kingdom did not know she was already learning.
