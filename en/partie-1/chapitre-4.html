The Lesson of the Ashes

This night does not belong to me entirely. The first half I owe to Rustum — the last words he left me, which I have carried ever since the way one carries a debt that can never be repaid.

I had already lost a battle before this one.

It was before Shabrān, at a time when I knew the man who would become our king only as one rider among others. I had fifteen men under my command and a village to protect, and I spent an entire night counting the enemies advancing toward us — their numbers, their ranks, the distance between us and them — convinced that if I calculated well enough, I would know when to strike and where. I calculated very well. I won the fight that I was fighting.

Meanwhile, a handful of them had slipped through the flank I had not counted and had set fire to the granaries of the village that I believed I was defending.

Weakened on that side, we had no choice but to fall back: the enemy, emboldened by that breakthrough, now dominated us as much by morale as by numbers. The battle cost us many dead and wounded; the village fell into the hands of its tormentors, and the mission was a bitter failure. I can still hear the cries of pain of my comrades in the tents where the wounded were being treated, hurling every curse they knew, as if their sacrifice had served for nothing.

No one ever blamed me for that night. I was even congratulated. But I learned, in the ashes of those granaries, that winning a fight and protecting people were not always the same thing, and that I had been wrong in my calculation from the start.

I never counted enemies the same way again. A few years later, I was serving in the cavalry alongside the man who would become our king — colleagues, then, each at the head of his own group. Then he climbed the ranks and took me along in his wake, perhaps because I no longer miscalculated. Together we had walked every hill, every path, every village around Shabrān, long before he took the throne and I became his son’s weapons master. That night, that knowledge of the terrain served me better than my sword.

The bell in the belfry rang out the alarm. I had heard it ring that way only once before, years earlier, during a drill. This time, no one was mistaken about it: it was not one.

I was in the training yard, alone, sharpening a blade that did not need it, when the first blow rang out. I did not run toward the palace. The king’s guards did not need me — there were a hundred of them, trained, paid for exactly this. Shaaban, though, had only me, and Shaaban, at that hour, was not at the palace. He was hanging around among the Common Folk, as always, wearing out his sandals on paving stones that were not his own.

I climbed the first rampart I came to, just long enough to see what it was that we were about to fight. What I saw tore away any hope of a quick calculation. These were not banners; they were a forest of banners — those of Sarhad, those of Kardun, and others I did not recognize, planted side by side as if their bearers had buried their quarrels for the length of a night. The neighboring lands had not sent an army. They had sent all their armies.

The eastern gate gave way before the guard had even had time to close its gratings. I saw it from afar: a movement, a lurch, then a black flood pouring into the breach like water through a broken dike. I did not stay to watch what came next. I had my student to find again, and the city between us changed its face with every new street I crossed.

I could not say how many men I passed that night before finding him again. Three, in a tanner’s yard, who were already looting what was left to loot — I did not take the time to ask them their intentions. Two more, farther on, who had cornered a family against the stone rim of a well — those I left on the ground and went on without a glance behind, because stopping would have cost a minute I did not have. One last, alone, who had the bad idea of blocking a lane too narrow for two men to pass each other.

Each fight cost me time that I counted in a single face: Shaaban’s, which I still could not find.

It was at the top of a sloping lane that I saw them: twenty figures or so, perhaps more, all running in the same direction, driven by the same fear, without looking back. One does not flee like that for nothing. I followed that wave rather than go on searching at random — if there was a place where the chaos gathered the defenseless, it was there, and Shaaban had always had a gift for ending up on the side of the defenseless.

I found him at the end of that wave, not in the middle of it. He had stopped — to help someone to their feet, I learned later, a detail that did not surprise me for a second — and that stop had been enough to leave him behind the rest of the crowd, alone, unarmed, facing men who had no reason to let him pass.

He had preferred to create a diversion rather than flee, to give the others time to find shelter. That young man I had watched grow up knew how to defend himself, believe me. Even unarmed, he dodged the assaults of those who wanted his life with a precision that, despite the distance that still separated us, filled my heart with immense pride. He had managed to lead his pursuers into another lane — but those men were smarter than he had hoped: they split up in mid-run to trap him better, at the very moment he reached a wall with no way out.

The first came out of a smashed-in door, without a sound. The second, from a recess I had not even noticed while running. The third jumped from a low wall.

Shaaban was cornered, backed against the stone. He was about to take the full force of the three men’s assault, and despite the guard he had already taken up to defend himself — brave, I can testify to it, not to give in to fear — their blades would make short work of him.

I arrived in time, on a quick slide that scraped the stone beneath my boots. I wounded the one facing him before he could strike, countered the two blades coming from the sides, and pushed my protégé behind me — at the price of a small cut received in passing, nothing more, I thought at the time.

The one I had hit in the thigh backed away limping, out of the fight for good. The other two remained, more cautious now, no longer charging but looking for an opening. I left them none: I disarmed the first with a move Shaaban had watched me repeat a hundred times in the training yard without ever seeing it work for real. The last took advantage of that half-second of inattention to slip his blade under my guard.

The iron entered under my ribs with an ease that nearly surprised me. I remember thinking, with a strange calm, that it was not the pain I had expected.

I still finished the one who had struck me — I could not say with what move. Then my legs stopped carrying me, and I fell to my knees in front of Shaaban rather than to the side.

“Rustum.”

I heard him say my name the way one calls to someone already moving away.

“Look at me,” I said. “Not at them.”

He looked at me. Blood was rising to my mouth, but my voice, strangely, remained that of the training yard — steady, unhurried, as if I were correcting a stance.

“I taught you to count enemies.” Each word cost me more than the last. “I made that mistake once, long ago, and I lost an entire village from counting too well.” I had almost no breath left to finish. “Tonight, stop counting enemies. Count the people you can still save. It is harder. And it is the only count worth making.”

I could not, and would not, say anything else. I closed my eyes before he could find a reply. I had one breath left, only one, and I gave all of it to one last sentence.

“I am proud of the man you have become.”

My hand let go of the blade.

The sky had caught fire from the projectiles moving across it — flaming arrows, stones torn from siege engines, tracing arcs of light above the burning city.

I saw the blade fall into the sand before I understood what it meant. Then Rustum’s silence fell on me all at once, heavier than his body against mine. I screamed his name — once, twice, until my voice broke — holding him against me as if noise alone could keep him a little longer.

I stayed kneeling for a time I could not measure, Rustum’s weight against me, his warm blood mixed, on my hands, with some other blood I could not name. Then I heard, very close, the scrape of a boot on stone — the two men he had not had time to finish off were closing in, certain of ending it quickly.

A torch, somewhere behind me, suddenly licked at an awning of dry wood, and that burst of light was enough to bring me back to myself — to a mind that kept replaying every moment spent at my master’s side: the joys, the sorrows we had lived through together, his lessons, his silences, even his anger at times, when I made mistakes in the training yard. A flame lit in my chest — not of rage, but the brutal certainty that I would never see him again, that I would never again walk at his side, never again laugh with him, never again suffer his wrath. Before I had even understood what was happening, my hand had closed on his blade, and I had already cut down the two men who remained, in one precise stroke, without a tremor.

He had died protecting me. So I was going to follow in my master’s footsteps, and put into practice the last lesson he had just taught me at the price of his life: the lives of others before my own.

I set out to find whatever my hand could still catch — survivors here and there — hoping to bring a little light into the chaos. There were dozens that night in need of an outstretched hand. I do not remember all of the faces. Some, however, will never leave me.

Carried along by my urge to rescue, I drew near a market stall whose rubble gave me the feeling it sheltered a human presence — a tremor under the planks, a breath perhaps. Moving the charred boards aside, I found Yasmine, a beam across her leg: the old seller of rugs and figs who had always paid me a little too much, ever since I was ten. I heaved the beam off her with my bare hands, not knowing where the strength came from, and carried her to an alley I knew was still standing. She recognized me despite the soot, and her lips formed my name without any sound coming out. I propped her against an intact wall, my scarf rolled beneath her head, and promised to come back — a promise I was not certain I could keep.

Farther on, in the courtyard of a half-collapsed well, I noticed burst waterskins scattered among the rubble, and I thought that whoever owned them had not had the luck to fall back in time before the surprise attack. It was while digging that I found Idris, the water carrier, still alive under the rubble, trying nonetheless to quench the thirst of two children who were not his own. I cleared a path for him to the cellars of the weavers’ quarter, the only ones I knew still held, striking down everything that stood between us and that door. Idris would not let go of my sleeve, as if I were the last solid thing in the city. When the door closed on them, I heard the children begin to cry — and I understood it was a good sign: one cries only when one knows oneself to be safe.

As for Nadir, I found him almost by chance, trapped under the overturned bellows of his master’s forge, a beam across his back. He recognized me before I recognized him: he had grown so much since our secret exchanges of blows — broad shoulders, forearms blackened by years at the bellows — that I could not have said who it was. “The kid who always helped,” he murmured, before losing consciousness in my arms as I pulled him out. He weighed heavy now, with everything those years at the forge had given him.

To cut through a quarter that patrols were combing, I climbed the wall-walk of a half-crumbling wall. From up there, the burning night showed me an inner courtyard that the smoke had not yet reached. I leapt from the gangway, rolled across the paving stones and rose in the shadow of the wall opposite — and there, I froze.

In the middle of the courtyard, some twenty people sat on the bare ground, wrists and ankles bound, lined up like goods about to be loaded for sale. No one was shouting. That silence frightened me more than all the screams of the night.

Something rang false in such order amid such disorder. I moved forward without a sound, hugging the walls, setting each step as if the ground itself might betray me, until I could make out their faces in the glow of the fires. One of them, bolder than the rest, finally whispered a few words to me, almost without moving his lips: soldiers had already taken a first group through the back door, a heavy wooden gate — and no one had come back. Behind the gate, something growled, very low, and I could not put a name to it.

Enemy patrols passed on the other side of the walls; I could hear their boots, their orders. I knew it already: I could not move all these people without being seen.

That was when the door opened, without warning.

Two soldiers came out, sent to fetch the next ones, unhurried, like men who had already done this a hundred times. Highly trained veterans, armored in iron from head to knee. In the depths of the night, they did not notice the shadow pressed against the wall. I struck the first at the exact spot Rustum had made me aim for ten thousand times on a straw dummy: the hollow between the gorget and the breastplate. The blade bit into the gap, but did not go deep enough. He screamed, but he stayed standing.

I had never struck a man before that night, and I was discovering the difference between a dummy and a veteran. The two wounded men in the alley, whom Rustum had already broken, had not prepared me for these.

The fight that followed lasted far longer than I would ever have believed. Two men hardened by war, protected by iron, against a young man who had known only the training yard: I took blows that filled my mouth with blood, I backed away, turned, slid, and each time I thought I could no longer lift my arm, Rustum’s voice came back into my head — the joints, the armpit, the back of the knee. The city burned loudly enough to cover the clash of steel: no one came. The first fell when I finally found the hollow beneath his arm. The second cost me a cut on the shoulder before my blow found the flaw in his armor.

I picked up their daggers without thinking, the way one picks up tools one might need. Then I cut the captives’ bonds, one after another, telling them to be ready.

It was while cutting the last rope that I understood what the growl meant. Behind the gate, they must have smelled the blood and heard the clash, and the soldiers were not coming back. The wood exploded under an impact, and two beasts burst through: war hounds, tall as calves, their coats matted with dried blood, muzzles red up to the eyes, jaws clenched, gazes fixed. I understood, at that instant, why no one had come back.

I threw myself between them and the captives. I struck the first on the muzzle, the second on the shoulder, hard enough that they looked at nothing but me. “The door!” I shouted to the captives. “It is the only way out!” The soldiers who guarded it were dead, and the beasts had left their post. They rushed into the passage behind the gate, jostling one another, helping each other up, and I saw nothing more than fangs.

The fight dragged on. I could not tell it in order: jaws snapping a breath from my throat, cobblestones slick with blood, my arms grown too heavy for Rustum’s blade. Each time I thought I had struck one of them, the other forced me back.

Fatigue crept in, sly, and for one instant, only one, I let my vigilance slip. The first leapt on me and pinned me to the ground. All I had for a rampart was Rustum’s blade, held crosswise in both hands, and its jaw working to get through. While it bit the iron, I grabbed one of the soldiers’ daggers and drove it to the hilt into its eye. It collapsed beside me, all at once.

I did not even have time to catch my breath. The second had already lunged, jaws wide, ready to swallow me whole. My concentration has never been as complete as in that instant: I dodged the jaws by a hair, planted the second dagger in its front leg, picked up Rustum’s blade, and drove it sideways into its head, in a single sequence, so fast that I understood what I had done only when I saw it fall.

Silence fell again over the courtyard. I stayed for a moment on my knees, hands open before me: they were trembling at last. The gate was wide open, and behind it, no one. I will never know whether the captives found their way out — they, the ones in the courtyard, and so many others whose names were lost in the smoke of that night.

I no longer counted the soldiers I came across. I counted the roofs I had to avoid, the hands I still had to catch before they vanished into the smoke, the faces I recognized and those I did not, which counted just as much.

That night, I did not know what had become of my father, or of Syrus, or of Baam. I did not know, either, where Ishirine was, or whether the palace was still standing. Already, morning was breaking on the horizon — and with it, its share of mysteries and battles still to come.
