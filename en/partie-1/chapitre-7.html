Part I · Chapter 7

The Duel of the Brothers

The new king was crowned six days after the end of the raid, on the main square, in front of the charred granaries. We had buried our dead the day before, and the city had not finished mourning them before it was already demanding someone to obey. The High Nobility filled the front rows, draped in whatever it had managed to save of its fine fabrics; behind it, the Common Folk stood pressed together in the ash, saying nothing, and it was they, I believe, who had made the choice. They had seen the man who would become king hold the alleys for three nights. They asked for nothing more.

Ishirine stood on my right, her back straight, her hands clasped behind her, her eyes fixed on the guards posted around the square.

Our father’s crown, which he had hardly ever worn, had not left its chest during the raid. The king received it from the hands of the oldest of the advisers, without a word, and the crowd, which had been holding its breath for days, let it out in a single cry of exaltation. He did not seem to hear it. He was looking at something above us all, very far away, like someone receiving a burden he has sworn never to set down. The one who had been our brother in the morning was no longer quite our brother by evening.

Then he raised his arm and pointed toward the horizon, where our lands ended.

“Never again,” he said, and that was his whole speech.

No one understood. The cries died all at once, and a feeling of unease ran through the crowd: people looked at each other, wondering whether he was speaking of the raid, of the future, of our dead, or of someone in particular. It was an old soldier, somewhere in the back rows, who broke the silence by shouting “Long live the king!”, and the cheers began again, louder than before, as if to drown out the question no one dared ask.

The very next day, he summoned the council in the wing of the palace that still had a roof. There was no preamble and no condolences. The map of the kingdom had been spread out on the table, and the stewards laid their bad news on it one after another: the burned granaries, meaning grain to be rationed from autumn; the gutted forges; the timber, the stone, the iron, everything we would have needed by the cartload and no longer had. The king listened, one finger resting on the villages of the east, which had stayed loyal and could supply timber, food, a few more hands; for the rest, we would have to buy, negotiate, wait for the caravans. He decided quickly, and his decisions held: by the end of the session, every sack, every beam and every man had found its place.

“The rebuilding will take a year,” he concluded. “A whole year, no more, no less. By the end of that time, there will not be one broken door left in this kingdom.”

I raised my hand, which I rarely did in those rooms. I asked that the first sacks of grain go to the lower quarters, before the barracks, before the noble houses.

“The first sacks will go to the Common Folk,” the king answered without hesitation. “It was already written.”

I remember feeling, that day, something that resembled hope. At the other end of the table, Ishirine said nothing. She was looking at the spot where the map still bore the mark of the cavalry stables, struck out with a cross.

When the session ended, Ishirine waited for the room to empty, then walked up to the table.

“I want to train with the new cavalry,” she said. “The stables burned, the captains are dead, and those who remain have no one left to teach them how to maneuver in formation.”

“No.” He had not looked up from the map. “The kingdom has buried enough of its own.”

She came back the next day, then three days later, each time with a new argument, never raising her voice. The second time, she spoke of the elite soldiers: most had fallen during the raid, those who remained could be counted on one hand, and yet it was to them that the neighboring lands looked to know whether Shabrān was still standing. “A kingdom without a face invites its neighbors to forget it, or to attack it,” she said. The king answered that a princess was not a banner. The third time, she laid on the table what she had prepared: a list of villages that no patrol visited anymore, with their distances. “People no longer believe in soldiers they cannot see. They will believe in a princess who brings their children back. If I fall, you lose one blade. If I return, you gain what neither gold nor iron has given you.”

The king looked at her for a long time. “You are more stubborn than Father,” he said at last. “You will start with the cavalry.”

She trained all winter, from dawn to night, with men recruited among the survivors and the sons of the lower quarters. In spring, she enlisted in the kingdom’s army. She was sent first to escort the convoys of the eastern villages, but that was not where she made her name. She excelled at liberation missions: prisoners torn from bandit chiefs, families taken back from their jailers, whole villages that had lived for years under the boot of their tormentors and that, one morning, had no one left to fear. Her reputation ran faster than her horses, within the kingdom and beyond our borders, and it took only a few months for her to be placed at the head of a group whose men she chose and whose missions she led. Each success gave back a little of its shine to the name of Shabrān.

But what the kingdom gained from it, she paid for, and I could see it. The girl I had known was lively, courteous, curious about everything; she laughed at meals, remembered the name of every maid, asked a thousand questions. The one who came back from missions spoke little. She had more courage than words, and she spent her evenings immersed in books on maps, laws, and the history of the lands rather than in anyone’s company. Of the girl she had been, only her grace remained, the way she tied her hair before mounting a horse, a femininity she seemed to keep in spite of herself. Everything else had changed, as if she had buried it with our brothers.

As for me, I did not stop for a single day. I carried stones at dawn, dug foundations at noon, roofed houses until night, in one quarter, then the next, then the one after, wherever hands were lacking. I handed out grain to the lines in front of the granaries, I raised the walls of houses that no one would rebuild without help, I carried the old on my back when they had to be moved. I rebuilt Yasmine’s stall, then the forge where Nadir had nearly died, then a hundred others that I never counted. The Common Folk ended up recognizing me by my step and leaving their doors open to me. Day after day, asking for nothing, because I did not know how to do otherwise. And when night fell, when my arms could take no more, I went down to the palace gardens, far from any eyes, and went back, alone, to my master’s lessons: the footing, the breathing, the exact moment to strike. I repeated them until my arms refused, because it was the only way I had of not letting his voice fade. The seasons passed like that, to the rhythm of carts and hammers, and sometimes I came to believe that the hardest part was behind us.

The rebuilding was finished a few weeks after the deadline the king had set, which no one dared remind him of. The council chamber had its doors back, the main square its paving stones, and the granaries, new, no longer smelled of ash. Then the king gathered the high dignitaries of every level: the High Nobility in its finest attire and, standing behind it, the elders of the lower quarters and those of the eastern villages. He laid the map on the table, as he had a year earlier, and drew three circles on it.

“Sarhad, Kardun, Nakhur,” he said. “Three lands, and behind them someone we have never seen. The arrows that finished the chief of Nakhur in our arena, no one knows from what hand they came; those who opened the eastern gate had plans that only neighbors could have supplied. A hand I cannot see is more dangerous than three I know, and I will not give it a second chance. These lands will pass under a single banner, one after another, and the next one who wants to strike us will find before him a single kingdom, and not neighbors who can be set against one another.”

There was a silence, then murmurs, then a few heads bowing. The High Nobility saw land. The Common Folk, I believe, saw above all their king, the one who had given them the first sacks of grain.

I stood up, my heart pounding harder than on the day of the raid.

“You speak of a banner,” I said. “I see villages. Women who have never carried weapons, towns without walls, peasants who have paid their tribute for generations.”

“Those who surrender will be fed,” the king answered. “I have promised nothing else.”

I stayed on my feet. “Our army lost its best men a year ago. You would send it against three lands?”

“Against three lands, one after another. Today’s army is not last year’s: it has learned, and at a high price.”

One last time, I spoke, louder than I should have. “If we annex them, those who hate us today will be ten times more numerous tomorrow.”

“They will not hate us for long,” the king answered, “once they have eaten at our table.”

No one responded. I looked around the room: some avoided my eyes, others sighed, the way one sighs before a child who has not understood. I sat back down, my jaw tight, irritated less by his arguments than by a decision he had made long before gathering us.

From the very next day, I began my rounds. I presented myself to every group in the kingdom, one after another, day after day: the northern garrison that Syrus had commanded, the guilds of smiths and weavers, the cavalry, the elders of the lower quarters, the palace’s men of law. I pleaded for each land, one by one. Sarhad had paid a light tribute for generations: its towns had neither armies nor ambitions, only strongholds and habits of neutrality. Kardun negotiated for its grain: war there would starve the poor long before the garrisons. And Nakhur, Bahram’s land, was a people who had already paid an immense debt — the death of its chief on our soil, in circumstances that no one had ever cleared up.

People listened to me politely, sometimes with sincere sympathy, and no one followed me. The men of the northern garrison had not forgotten who had taken their commander from them. The guilds were already counting the weapons they would be asked to forge. As for the elders of the lower quarters, they answered me, with an embarrassment I will never forget: “You do not argue with the one who feeds you.” No group supported me. I finally understood that I was not asking them to choose between two ideas, but between their king and me.

I went back to see the king, once, then ten times. He always received me, listened to me to the end, and answered the same thing with the same patience, which was worse than a refusal.

One evening that same week — it was raining, I remember, a fine, stubborn rain that would not end — I went to find him one last time. He was alone in the council chamber, the map still spread out, an oil lamp burning low, his spear leaning against the wall. I think he knew why I was there, and I also think, looking back, that he had been expecting this visit for days.

“Give me a reason to listen to you,” he said without looking up.

“Because everyone tells you yes, and I am the only one who can tell you no without wanting anything from you.”

He looked up then. And what was in his eyes was not anger — it was something more worn, more intimate. Weariness, perhaps, that of a man who no longer has the right to doubt.

“If you believe so strongly in what you say,” he said at last, “prove it.”

“Prove it how?”

“The way we settle everything, between us.”

He stood up, took his spear, and planted its butt in the flagstones, the shaft upright, trembling. “If you defeat me, I will listen to what you have to say, and the council will listen to you too. You will want to plead for Sarhad, for Kardun, for every fig tree in every town — you will have earned that right. But if you lose, you stop. You stop going to my captains, you stop quoting your master to my soldiers, you stop making those who need certainty in order to fight begin to doubt. You let me wage this war without my own house contesting it.”

I should have refused. I knew it, even then: a kingdom is not governed at the price of a duel, and my victory would have changed nothing about the maps or the numbers. But he was offering me the one thing I have never been able to refuse — a chance, however slim, to weigh on what was going to happen behind the walls of others.

“Not here,” the king added. “Neither before the court nor before the soldiers. Neither the prince who challenges his king, nor the king who raises his spear against his brother: no one needs to see that. In the gardens.”

He let me choose my weapon among those handed to recruits, an ordinary sword, without name or history, and he waited without impatience while I found the one that felt right. Then we went down into the gardens, under the rain.

The palace gardens stretched behind the stewards’ wing, a square of cypress and boxwood that the reconstruction had spared because there was nothing to rebuild there. The pool at the center, dry since the raid, was filling by itself with rain, and on the west side a gallery of pillars plunged into shadow. There were no torches and no guards: the king had sent his escort back to the foot of the stairs, and the only lights were those of distant windows, trembling in the water.

“Until you drop your weapon,” he said, taking his stance, “or tell me that it is enough.”

He held his spear in one hand, the point low, the other arm hanging loose, with the calm of those who have nothing left to prove. My recruit’s sword reached only a third as far as his spear, and I understood from the first pass what that meant: every time I advanced, the point found me before I found it. He did not try to strike. He kept me at a distance, the way one keeps a runaway horse, and he let me wear myself out against that distance for a while before speaking.

“You think I want this war,” he said, without taking his eyes off me. “I do not. I spent a year laying stones, like you. But I also spent that year thinking about the night of the raid, about what we saw and what we did not see. Someone struck us without our knowing who, or why, or how far they meant to go, and that danger did not vanish with the bodies. I cannot see its face. I only feel that it is still watching us. I left my weapon with Father, Shaaban, and I came back too late. So yes, I would rather be hated by neighbors than mourned by my people.”

“You are chasing a hand you cannot see,” I answered, short of breath, “and you strike the ones you can. Sarhad fired no arrow. Kardun burned no granary. They are weavers, shepherds, old women who sell figs, and you want to teach them who you are by taking away what they have. You cannot disarm a fear by feeding it. Every village you take gives you one more enemy inside your own kingdom, and whoever fired those arrows, whoever he is, will only have to wait for you to weaken yourself.”

The shaft struck my forearm before I saw the blow start, the point opened my skin in passing, and the mud did the rest. I fell. The king did not strike a man on the ground. He lowered his spear and waited, rain running down his face.

“You can believe what you want,” he said while I was getting up. “You are the fourth. No one ever expected anything of you, and that is what gave you time to love people one by one, as one can when one does not have to choose. I had to learn to love them by the thousands, and one does not love thousands of people the way one loves one. One protects them, that is all, and one accepts that some will not understand why. If I am wrong, I will carry that weight. If I am right, you will never know how many lives I will have spared you.”

“I am not asking you to love me,” I said, blood warm in my sleeve. “I am asking you to hear yourself. You speak of thousands of people you protect, and you know these lands better than I do: you traveled them while I grew up among the market stalls. You know better than anyone what an army leaves behind when it passes, and that is why I cannot let you do it. I do not know how to stand and watch. I never have. If the army leaves, I will put myself in its way, even uselessly, because I would rather be an obstacle that serves no purpose than the helpless witness of a massacre.”

Then I did what my master had taught me: the footing, the breathing, the exact moment. The point, pulled back by a breath, left an opening, and I threw myself into it. The king had foreseen it. The shaft turned, the butt of the spear struck me at the temple, and I felt my brow split open before I heard the sound. I fell a second time, and I got up.

I fell a third time, my breath cut off by a blow of the shaft in the ribs. I stayed a moment on my knees, my mouth full of rain.

“No one will blame you for having held out this long,” said the king. “Stay there.”

“It is not so that no one will blame me that I hold on.”

He nodded, slowly, the way one acknowledges an answer one was expecting. Then something changed in the way he stood. For the first time that night, he took his spear in both hands.

The first attacks were too fast for me to count. I deflected two. The third slipped under my guard, the shaft swept my legs, and the butt of the spear struck me between the shoulder blades with all the force of a man holding nothing back. I fell face-first into the mud of the pool, with the taste of iron in my mouth. For a moment, I no longer knew which way was up or down.

I got up. Not once, all night, did my blade find his flesh.

“Say that it is enough,” he said. There was no hardness left in his voice, only weariness. “You have lost, Shaaban. You know it. Keep your word.”

“I have not lost as long as I get up.”

I tried a fifth time. I brought one foot under me, then, leaning on my blade, I struggled to lift my second knee out of the mud, my body no longer anything but a wound that stood upright out of habit. The king faced me, his spear at rest, without his guard, waiting for me to be on my feet before resuming.

“Enough.”

The voice rang out under the gallery. Ishirine was leaning against one of the pillars, arms crossed, eyes closed, her traveling armor covered in mud up to the shoulders. I had not seen her arrive. She told me later that she was coming back from a ten-day mission, that she dreamed of nothing but a bed, and that she had stopped under the gallery when she recognized the sound of steel. She had not reopened her eyes: she had counted my falls by the sound.

“Four times, Shaaban. I counted them. He has not hit you once while you were down, and he is still waiting for you without his guard, because he does not want to kill you and does not know how else to stop you. You, you do not know how to lose, so you get up. It will not change anything tonight, or tomorrow. He will not listen to you because you bleed. The kingdom needs you alive, even against him, and your voice is worth nothing in this mud. The day you are able to beat him, he will listen to you, and so will everyone who follows him. Not before.”

She fell silent and opened her eyes. For a long moment, no one moved. Ishirine and the king looked at each other without a word, the rain falling between them like a curtain, and I never knew what passed in that silence. I only know that it seemed to last longer than all the rest of the duel. Then the king planted his spear in the ground, as if to say that the night was over, and turned his back on me. He did not look back.

Ishirine unfolded her arms and left her pillar to come toward me.

I tried to get up to say something, I no longer know what. My second knee left the mud, found it again, and all the strength that had held me up that night drained away at once, without a sound. I collapsed. The last face I saw was Ishirine’s, leaning over me, and the rain ran into my mouth. The garden went dark from the edges, like a lamp being turned down.
