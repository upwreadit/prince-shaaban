Part I · Chapter 8

The Open Road

About 7 minutes’ reading

I slept for a long time. Ten days, I was told, give or take. When I was able to get up, the city had already caught its breath: the caravans from the east were coming down to the market again, the forges were ringing, children were running across the paving stones of the main square, and nothing in the streets bore the trace of what had been decided without me. The army had been gone for days. Shabrān was alive. It was almost obscene, the way life went on without asking permission from those who had lost it.

I was told that the king had come to see me in the evenings, whenever the council meetings left him free, that he stood at the foot of the bed without a word and left again before dawn. Ishirine, for her part, had not left the palace. She had not even worn armor since I had been asleep, only plain soldier’s clothes, and she, who had not sat still for a year, had not taken a single mission, not even the shortest one.

She was sitting by the window when I opened my eyes, a book on her lap that she was not reading.

“You are staying with him,” I said when I found my voice again, and it was not a question.

“I’m staying.” She explained, without justifying herself, that she could do more here than elsewhere: train men, command missions, tear prisoners away from their jailers. She added that someone had to hold the kingdom while the army was out, and that for that she was worth more than any adviser. She did not ask whether I agreed.

I went to find the king the following evening, in the small map room at the top of the east wing, where no one went up unless invited. He was sitting at the table, alone, his chin in his hand, an oil lamp burning low beside the map he was studying. He did not look up when I pushed the door open. He knew it was me.

“I am leaving,” I said.

He ran a finger over a point on the map before answering. “You gave your word.”

“I have not lost.”

“No. You did not surrender.” At last he turned his head toward me. “Then tell me how you intend to keep it.”

“I will not contest you here anymore. Not before your captains, not before your court, not before the least soldier. I will keep my word by going away.”

He rolled up the map, starting with the edge facing me, without hurrying. “Let us walk,” he said.

We went down the stairs side by side, then crossed the galleries without an escort, our footsteps echoing alone under the vaults.

“And out there?” he asked.

“Out there, I will do what I know how to do. I will lift up those your army leaves behind.”

“It will leave only what I have decided to leave.”

“That is precisely what worries me.”

He did not answer. I think he almost smiled.

We reached the gardens without exchanging another word. The spear was still there, planted at the edge of the pool, which the rain had finished filling, its shaft glistening with dew, like those dead trees seen around the palace after the raid. No one had dared touch it. The king pulled it from the ground in a single motion, the way one pulls out a thorn, and held it out to me by the middle of the shaft.

“Are you giving it to me, or lending it?”

“I am lending it to you. I intend to get it back.”

Then he walked me to the stables, on foot, adding nothing, and led me to the last stall, the one at the very back. The horse inside was already saddled, broad, calm, a deep black with no gleam, and its bedding was fresh, as if it had been prepared for someone expected.

“You had already chosen it.”

“It was waiting.” He tightened the girth himself, once, then a second time, without looking at me. I let it pass. I took it for a king’s whim, or one last provocation. It took me a long time to understand that it was something else.

When I mounted, the spear secured to my saddle, he laid his hand on the horse’s neck, for a second, as if to make sure it would hold.

“Try to stay alive until we meet again,” he said. “And next time, try to entertain me more.”

“Do not worry about me, brother. Next time, you will not be bored.” It was the first time since the coronation that I had called him that. He did not answer, and I did not wait for him to.

So I took my leave of the king and set off at a slow canter across the courtyard, the spear resting against my stirrup.

Ishirine was waiting for me at the palace gates. She had seen me long before, from the gallery, when the king had led me to the stables, spear in hand, and she had understood without being told anything: one does not give a spear and a horse to someone who is staying. She stood in the middle of the passage, in plain soldier’s clothes, her arms at her sides. I dismounted. We looked at each other for a long time, without a word. Then she smiled, a wide smile, the first I had seen on her since the raid, perhaps since before. She held me against her for a long time, and I returned her embrace. I did not ask her to come. She did not offer. She told me later that she had stayed there, motionless, until the alleys had swallowed me up again.

The way from the gates of the city ran through the market. I took it without slowing down. The stalls were opening, voices I knew were calling out to the first customers, and someone, far behind, shouted my name. I did not turn my head. Stopping to say goodbye would have cost me no more than the time it takes to walk ten steps, but what for? One does not say goodbye to what one has already left. What I had to give them, I had given, stone after stone, for a year.

At the gates of the city, an old man was sitting on a milestone, both hands resting on a new cane, its wood still pale, as if he were waiting for me or for anyone. He watched me pass, then stopped me with a word.

“You gave me water, that night. Against a wall, on the way to the palace.”

I did not remember. I told him so, and he gave a short, dry laugh. “That is the best way to give.”

We talked for a moment, not much. He asked me where I was going.

“Where the road ends,” I answered, “and a little beyond.”

“Then here are two pieces of advice, since you gave me water. I walked these roads for thirty years before my knees brought me back here. Never drink from a well that the birds avoid. And give your name only to those who have already given you theirs.”

He undid a knot in his belt and placed in my hand an object wrapped in wool, light and warm like a pebble left in the sun. “There is no other like it. Do not open it before you need it.” I did not insist. He took his cane back in both hands, and I rode on.

Behind me, the gates of Shabrān closed without a sound. I did not hear the bolt; I felt it. Ahead of me, the road stretched out, rutted, still warm from what the army had carried off. I did not know what I would find there, nor what it would cost me to gather up, behind my own brother, what he left in his wake, nor how many doors would close in front of me because of it. I knew only that, somewhere beyond the horizon taking shape at the end of the road, my presence, my skills, everything I was might be of use to others.
